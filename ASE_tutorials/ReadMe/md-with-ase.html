Molecular dynamics, from Newton to ASE

A twenty-three episode pathway for absolute beginners: people who can write a Python loop but have never watched a set of atoms move. Every concept is earned before the library hides it, and every line of ASE code below was run against ASE 3.29.0 before it was written down.

A

Before you press record

Two failure modes kill teaching channels in the first month: rebuilding the environment on camera every episode, and discovering mid-take that you don't know what the viewer already knows. Fix both once, here.

What the viewer must already have

  • Python loops, functions, lists — nothing more
  • NumPy arrays and slicing (you'll re-teach broadcasting)
  • School-level vectors and F = ma
  • No thermodynamics, no statistical mechanics, no prior MD

The stack you standardise on

  • conda create -n asemd python=3.11
  • pip install ase numpy matplotlib jupyterlab
  • ASE 3.29.0 — pin it and say the version on screen
  • Jupyter for episodes 1–12, plain .py scripts from 13 on

Systems you never change

  • Argon, Lennard-Jones — the whole theory arc
  • Copper, EMT — anything metallic or solid-state
  • Both ship with ASE, so viewers never chase a potential file
  • One system per concept means the plots are comparable across episodes
B

The pathway

Phases are ordered by dependency, not by difficulty. Nothing in a later phase needs anything you haven't already built on camera. Each episode lists the idea, the code you type live, the demo that makes it click, and what the viewer does alone before the next upload.

Phase 0

What are we even doing

2 eps · ~35 min

Nobody finishes a 23-part course they don't yet want. Spend two episodes buying their attention.

01
18 min

What a molecular dynamics simulation actually is

Idea
MD is one loop: compute forces, move atoms, repeat. Everything else — thermostats, ensembles, free energies — is bookkeeping around that loop. Show that argon melts, that a crystal has an RDF and a liquid doesn't, and that these came out of Newton's laws and nothing else.
On screen
No code. Play a rendered argon melting trajectory, then a running solid, then the two g(r) curves side by side.
The hook
Put the four-line pseudocode of the MD loop on one slide and say: "by episode 10 you will have written every line of this yourself, and you will know why each one is there."
Homework
Install the environment. That's it.
02
16 min

Setting up, and meeting the Atoms object

Idea
ASE's whole design in one sentence: an Atoms object holds what and where, a calculator answers how much energy and force, and a dynamics object decides what moves next. Nothing else to learn.
On screen
conda env from scratch, pip install ase, then from ase.build import molecule, bulk, inspect .positions, .get_chemical_symbols(), .cell, and view(atoms).
The hook
Build a water molecule and a copper crystal in four lines each, rotate them in the ASE GUI. Instant payoff, zero theory.
Homework
Build three structures of their choosing and post a screenshot in the comments.
Phase 1

MD from first principles — no library allowed

5 eps · ~1h 45m

This is the phase that separates your channel from every "here's a script, run it" tutorial. NumPy only. When ASE arrives in Phase 2 the viewer already knows what it is doing for them.

03
22 min

Potential energy surfaces and where forces come from

Idea
Energy is a function of all the coordinates. Force is minus its gradient. The Lennard-Jones form, why r−12 and r−6, what ε and σ physically mean.
On screen
Plot LJ energy and its analytic derivative for two argon atoms. Write lj_energy(r) and lj_force(r) in ten lines. Verify the analytic force against a finite difference.
The hook
Slide two atoms together and watch the force flip sign at the minimum. That single plot is the physical intuition for the rest of the series.
Homework
Repeat for the Morse potential and compare the well shapes.
04
24 min

Integrating Newton: why velocity Verlet and not Euler

Idea
Discretising F = ma. Forward Euler, its energy blow-up, then velocity Verlet: half-kick, drift, force, half-kick. Time-reversibility and symplecticity in plain words — no proofs.
On screen
Both integrators for a 1-D harmonic oscillator, ~30 lines of NumPy. Plot total energy against time for each.
The hook
Euler's energy climbs off the top of the plot while Verlet's oscillates around a flat line — at identical timestep and identical cost. The viewer never asks "why Verlet?" again.
Homework
Extend to two LJ atoms in 3-D using the force from episode 3.
05
18 min

Choosing a timestep, and the femtosecond

Idea
The timestep must resolve the fastest vibration in the system. The rule of thumb: roughly one tenth of the shortest period. Why that is ~1 fs for anything with hydrogen and can be 5 fs for heavy noble gases.
On screen
Run the same oscillator at increasing dt until it explodes. Then measure energy drift per atom as a function of dt.
The hook
The drift table from a real copper run (verified, see Appendix C): 0.006 → 0.141 → 0.395 meV per atom as dt goes 1 → 5 → 10 fs. Drift is a number you measure, not a vibe.
Homework
Find the timestep at which their own two-atom code loses 1 meV/atom over 1 ps.
06
21 min

Temperature is not a knob — it's a measurement

Idea
Equipartition: ⟨KE⟩ = (3/2)N kBT, so temperature is read out of the velocities. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and why a fresh simulation loses half its temperature in the first picosecond.
On screen
Draw velocities from a Gaussian, histogram the speeds against the analytic Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, compute instantaneous T, watch it fluctuate.
The hook
Initialise at 300 K, run 200 steps, measure 150 K. Explain equipartition between kinetic and potential energy — the single most common beginner confusion, defused before they ever hit it.
Homework
Show that instantaneous T fluctuates less as N grows.
07
20 min

Periodic boundaries and the minimum image convention

Idea
A hundred atoms in a vacuum is a droplet, not a bulk. PBC removes the surface. The minimum image convention, the cutoff radius, and the hard rule: rcut must be less than half the shortest box length.
On screen
Wrap coordinates by hand, implement minimum-image distance in three lines, draw the 2-D tiled box.
The hook
Trigger ASE's real error live — CellTooSmall: the cell is not large enough. Viewers who meet an error with you don't fear it alone at 2 a.m.
Homework
Given a cubic box of 15.78 Å, what is the largest legal rmax for an RDF? (Answer: 7.89 Å.)
Phase 2

ASE takes over the loop

5 eps · ~1h 40m

Now every ASE call maps onto something the viewer already wrote by hand. This is the phase where the series stops being a physics course and becomes a tool course.

08
20 min

The Atoms object, properly

Idea
positions, cell, pbc, numbers, momenta — five arrays that fully define a simulation state. Slicing, repeating, constraints, and the ase.build module.
On screen
bulk('Ar', 'fcc', a=5.26, cubic=True) * (3,3,3); inspect every array; use fcc111 to make a surface; save and reload with ase.io.write/read.
The hook
Show that atoms * (3,3,3) replaces the fifty-line supercell builder they were about to write.
Homework

09
19 min

Calculators: the one interface that runs everything

Idea
A calculator is a contract: give it an Atoms, ask for energy, forces, stress. LJ, EMT, DFT codes, and machine-learned potentials all satisfy the same contract — which is why swapping physics is a one-line change.
On screen
Attach LennardJones, then EMT; call get_potential_energy(), get_forces(), get_stress(). Show that results are cached until positions change.
The hook
Compare your hand-written episode-3 LJ force against ASE's LennardJones for the same geometry. They agree to machine precision. The library is not magic — it is your code, tested.
Homework
List which ASE calculators are installed on their machine and which need external binaries.
10
17 min

Your first real MD run — NVE in fifteen lines

Idea
The payoff episode. Structure → calculator → initial velocities → integrator → run. Constant energy, constant volume, constant number: the NVE ensemble.
On screen
Appendix A, verbatim, typed live. thermalize_momenta, VelocityVerlet, dyn.run(200), plus ase.units.fs and why ASE's internal time unit is not femtoseconds.
The hook
Fifteen lines reproduce what took the whole of Phase 1 by hand. Say so out loud — this is the emotional peak of the series.
Homework
Run the same script for argon and for copper; compare the energy drift.
11
21 min

Instrumenting a run: observers, loggers, trajectories

Idea
A simulation you can't inspect is a simulation you can't trust. ASE's observer pattern: dyn.attach(callable, interval=n). Anything callable works, including your own function.
On screen
MDLogger writing md.log, Trajectory writing .traj, then a custom observer that prints a warning when T drifts beyond a band.
The hook
Attach a live matplotlib callback that redraws the temperature trace every 20 steps. Watching the run breathe on screen is the most re-watched moment you will produce.
Homework
Write an observer that dumps a snapshot only when the potential energy hits a new minimum.
12
22 min

Relax before you run: optimisers and starting structures

Idea
A badly built structure has enormous forces, so MD converts them into enormous heat and the run is garbage. Geometry optimisation (BFGS, FIRE) versus dynamics: one seeks the minimum, the other samples around it.
On screen
Deliberately place two atoms 0.5 Å apart, run MD, watch it explode. Then relax with BFGS(atoms).run(fmax=0.05) and rerun cleanly.
The hook
The explosion. Show the failure first, name it, then fix it — the sequence viewers remember when it happens to them.
Homework
Relax a Cu surface slab and report the change in the top interlayer spacing.
Phase 3

Ensembles: controlling temperature and pressure

4 eps · ~1h 25m

The phase where most beginners silently produce wrong results — wrong ensemble, wrong friction, no equilibration. Teach the failure modes as hard as the API.

13
20 min

NVE, NVT, NPT — what an ensemble actually means

Idea
An ensemble is the set of microstates your simulation is allowed to visit. NVE conserves energy but not temperature; NVT lets energy flow to a bath; NPT lets the box breathe. Which one matches which experiment.
On screen
The same argon system in all three, three temperature traces on one plot.
The hook
State the rule they'll carry forever: "choose the ensemble that matches how your experiment is controlled, not the one that's easiest to run."
Homework
Which ensemble for a sealed autoclave? For an open crucible? Answer in the comments.
14
25 min

Thermostats in ASE, and how to pick one

Idea
Four families, four trade-offs: Langevin (stochastic, robust, kills real dynamics), NVTBerendsen (fast equilibration, wrong distribution), Bussi (correct canonical, gentle), NoseHooverChainNVT (deterministic, correct, needs tuning). What friction and taut physically do.
On screen
The same system under all four; plot the temperature distribution against the analytic canonical width.
The hook
Sweep Langevin friction over three orders of magnitude. Too weak and it never equilibrates; too strong and the atoms stop diffusing. Then say plainly: never measure a diffusion coefficient under a strong Langevin thermostat.
Homework
Find the friction at which their argon's diffusion coefficient starts to drop.
15
22 min

Barostats and NPT: letting the box breathe

Idea
Pressure from the virial and the stress tensor. Isotropic versus fully flexible cells. In ASE 3.29 the modern route is IsotropicMTKNPT / MTKNPT; the old ase.md.npt.NPT now lives at ase.md.melchionna.MelchionnaNPT.
On screen
NPT copper at several temperatures; plot the cell volume; extract the thermal expansion coefficient.
The hook
A number they can check against a handbook. Nothing builds trust in a simulation like reproducing a measured material property.
Homework
Repeat for argon and explain why the expansion is so much larger.
16
18 min

Equilibration versus production, and how to tell them apart

Idea
The first part of every trajectory is memory of your initial guess and must be discarded. Practical tests: running averages that flatten, forward-and-reverse averages that agree, block averages that stop shifting.
On screen
Plot potential energy against time from a deliberately bad start; mark the equilibration cut; recompute averages on both sides of it.
The hook
Compute the same average with and without discarding equilibration. The two numbers differ enough to change a conclusion.
Homework
Justify an equilibration cut for their own run with a plot, not a guess.
Phase 4

Getting numbers out of a trajectory

4 eps · ~1h 25m

Running MD is easy. Extracting a defensible number with an error bar is the actual skill, and almost no free tutorial teaches it.

17
20 min

Reading trajectories, averaging, and error bars

Idea
Consecutive MD frames are correlated, so the naive standard error is far too small. Block averaging, correlation time, and why "N = 10,000 steps" is not N = 10,000 samples.
On screen
read('md.traj', index=':'); loop frames; write a ten-line block-averaging function; show the error bar growing to its true value as block size increases.
The hook
An error bar that grows by 5× when computed correctly. This is the episode that makes researchers subscribe.
Homework
Report their mean potential energy with a block-averaged uncertainty.
18
22 min

Structure: the radial distribution function

Idea
What g(r) counts, why it goes to 1 at large r, and how to read solid, liquid and gas off its shape. Coordination number as the integral of the first peak.
On screen
Appendix B. from ase.geometry.rdf import get_rdf — since ASE 3.28 it takes a whole list of frames and averages for you. Hit the CellTooSmall error on purpose and fix it.
The hook
Overlay g(r) at 40 K and 120 K. Sharp peaks collapse into a liquid's broad first shell. You can see melting in a single plot.
Homework
Integrate the first peak and recover the fcc coordination number of 12.
19
22 min

Dynamics: mean squared displacement and diffusion

Idea
MSD versus time: ballistic at short times, linear at long times. The Einstein relation ⟨r²⟩ = 6Dt. Why a solid's MSD plateaus and a liquid's does not, and the trap of unwrapping coordinates across PBC.
On screen
MSD by hand from the trajectory, then ASE's DiffusionCoefficient for comparison. Fit only the linear regime and say why.
The hook
Same plot for solid and liquid argon. A flat line versus a straight climb — the cleanest definition of "solid" a beginner will ever see.
Homework
Extract D at three temperatures and make an Arrhenius plot.

Mini-project: melt a crystal, start to finish

One script, one notebook, one figure with four panels. Narrate the decisions, not the syntax.

When ASE isn't the engine: LAMMPS, VASP, and the handoff

Idea
ASE integrating in Python is fine for thousands of atoms, wrong for millions. Its two other roles: writing input files for a production code, and driving one over a socket. Choosing between them.
On screen

Reproducibility, and what to actually report

Idea

C

Verified code

These three snippets were executed against ASE 3.29.0 on Python 3.11 before being written here. Type them on screen exactly as they appear; they are the spine of episodes 10, 11 and 18.

Appendix A — episode 10NVE, the smallest complete MD run
from ase.build import bulk
from ase.calculators.emt import EMT
from ase.md.velocitydistribution import thermalize_momenta
from ase.md.verlet import VelocityVerlet
from ase import units

atoms = bulk('Cu', 'fcc', a=3.61, cubic=True) * (2, 2, 2)
atoms.calc = EMT()

thermalize_momenta(atoms, temperature_K=300)

dyn = VelocityVerlet(atoms, timestep=1.0 * units.fs)
dyn.attach(lambda: print(f"E = {atoms.get_total_energy():.4f} eV   "
                         f"T = {atoms.get_temperature():.1f} K"),
           interval=10)
dyn.run(200)
Appendix B — episodes 11 & 18NVT argon, logged, saved, and analysed
import numpy as np
from ase.build import bulk
from ase.calculators.lj import LennardJones
from ase.constraints import FixCom
from ase.md.velocitydistribution import thermalize_momenta
from ase.md.langevin import Langevin
from ase.md import MDLogger
from ase.io.trajectory import Trajectory
from ase.io import read
from ase.geometry.rdf import get_rdf
from ase import units

atoms = bulk('Ar', 'fcc', a=5.26, cubic=True) * (3, 3, 3)
atoms.calc = LennardJones(epsilon=0.0104, sigma=3.40, rc=10.0)
atoms.set_constraint(FixCom())          # modern replacement for fixcm=True
thermalize_momenta(atoms, temperature_K=90)

dyn = Langevin(atoms, timestep=5.0 * units.fs, temperature_K=90,
               friction=0.01 / units.fs, fixcm=False)

dyn.attach(MDLogger(dyn, atoms, 'md.log', header=True,
                    peratom=True, mode='w'), interval=20)
traj = Trajectory('ar.traj', 'w', atoms)
dyn.attach(traj.write, interval=20)

dyn.run(400)
traj.close()

frames = read('ar.traj', index='5:')    # drop equilibration
g, r = get_rdf(frames, rmax=7.0, nbins=140)
print("first peak at r =", round(float(r[np.argmax(g)]), 2), "A")
# -> first peak at r = 3.62 A
Appendix C — episode 5Energy drift as a function of timestep
import numpy as np
from ase.build import bulk
from ase.calculators.emt import EMT
from ase.md.velocitydistribution import thermalize_momenta
from ase.md.verlet import VelocityVerlet
from ase import units

for dt in [1.0, 5.0, 10.0]:
    a = bulk('Cu', 'fcc', a=3.61, cubic=True) * (2, 2, 2)
    a.calc = EMT()
    thermalize_momenta(a, temperature_K=300, rng=np.random.RandomState(0))
    e0 = a.get_total_energy()
    VelocityVerlet(a, timestep=dt * units.fs).run(200)
    drift = (a.get_total_energy() - e0) / len(a) * 1000
    print(f"dt = {dt:5.1f} fs   drift = {drift:8.3f} meV/atom")

# dt =   1.0 fs   drift =    0.006 meV/atom
# dt =   5.0 fs   drift =    0.141 meV/atom
# dt =  10.0 fs   drift =    0.395 meV/atom
D

API changes that will date your videos

Recent ASE releases renamed several of the functions every older MD tutorial on YouTube still uses. If you teach the old names your videos are stale on upload; if you teach the new ones and mention the old, you become the tutorial people find when their copied script warns at them.

DeprecatedUse insteadSay this on camera
MaxwellBoltzmannDistribution(...) thermalize_momenta(...) Same physics, added in 3.29. Every older tutorial uses the old name — mention it so viewers recognise it in the wild.
Langevin(..., fixcm=True) FixCom() constraint + fixcm=False Deprecated in 3.28 because fixcm=True does not sample the correct NVT distribution — noticeable in small systems.
ase.md.npt.NPT MelchionnaNPT, or IsotropicMTKNPT Moved to ase.md.melchionna. Prefer the newer MTK barostats in ase.md.nose_hoover_chain.
Analysis(...).get_rdf(...) ase.geometry.rdf.get_rdf(...) Since 3.28 it accepts a list of frames and averages over them. Partial RDFs were also fixed in 3.27 to match LAMMPS conventions.
Put the version on screen in every episode. A one-line print(ase.__version__) in the first cell costs three seconds and saves you a hundred "this doesn't work for me" comments. Pin the current version in each description and add a comment when something breaks.

Production notes

Recording

  • Editor font at 18–20 pt minimum — most viewers are on a phone
  • Light theme records better than dark for legibility at low bitrate
  • 1080p is enough; a clean microphone matters ten times more than resolution
  • OBS with two scenes: full-screen code, and slides with a webcam corner
  • Type the code live. Pasting loses the viewer's hands and their attention

The repository

  • One public repo, one folder per episode: ep03_lj_forces/
  • environment.yml at the root, pinned, tested on a clean machine
  • Every episode folder ships the finished script and a stub with TODOs for the homework
  • Link the exact folder in the first line of the description

Titles and discovery

  • Front-load the search term: "Molecular Dynamics in Python — Velocity Verlet from Scratch (ASE #4)"
  • Series number goes at the end, never the start — "#4" as a title opener kills click-through from search
  • One consistent thumbnail template: episode number large, one plot, three words maximum
  • Episodes 3, 10, 14, 18 and 19 answer questions people actively search for. Those are your entry points — make them strong enough to stand alone

Cadence and sequencing

  • Record Phases 0–2 (episodes 1–12) fully before publishing episode 1
  • Then publish weekly, same day, while recording Phases 3–5
  • A buffer of ten finished episodes is what survives a conference week or a paper deadline
  • Pin a comment on episode 1 with the full pathway so viewers can see where it goes

If you can only make ten episodes, make 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 14 and 18. That subset still takes an absolute beginner from nothing to a correctly thermostatted simulation with a structural observable — and it's a complete, finishable thing rather than an abandoned course.
