Fourteen ASE drills

Everything you need to know before you can teach ASE, as fourteen short exercises with a number to check yourself against. Each checkpoint below was produced by actually running the drill on ASE 3.29.0 — if your answer matches, you got it right for the right reason.

A

How to work through these

You are learning this in order to explain it. That changes the method: understanding a script well enough to run it is a much lower bar than understanding it well enough to answer a stranger's question about it in the comments.

Type it, never paste it

You will type this code on camera. Muscle memory for atoms.calc = EMT() is worth more now than saved minutes.

Predict before you run

Write down the number you expect, then run. Every gap between prediction and output is exactly the misconception a beginner will have.

Read docstrings, not tutorials

help(bulk) and bulk?? in Jupyter. ASE's source is short and readable — going there once beats searching ten times.

Keep a failure log

Every error you hit is an episode beat. Screenshot the traceback and note what fixed it — that file becomes your teaching gold.

B

The drills

Roughly eleven hours of work, comfortable across two or three weeks. Do them in order — each tier assumes the one above it. Tick them off as you go; your progress is remembered in this browser.

Tier 1

The Atoms object

4 drills · ~2 hrs

Before any physics: an ASE simulation is five NumPy arrays in a trench coat. Get fluent with them and two thirds of your future confusion never happens.

01
Build a water molecule and measure it

Make an H2O molecule, print its positions array, then measure the O–H bond length and the H–O–H angle from the coordinates — first by hand with NumPy, then with ASE's built-in methods. They must agree.

Look up ase.build.moleculeatoms.positionsget_distanceget_anglease.visualize.view

Checkpoint

3 atoms · O–H = 0.969 Å · H–O–H = 104.0°

If you're stuck

Atom 0 is oxygen, atoms 1 and 2 are the hydrogens. get_angle(1, 0, 2) — the middle index is the vertex.

By hand: np.linalg.norm(pos[1] - pos[0]), and the angle from np.arccos of the normalised dot product of the two O–H vectors.

Why this is drill oneEvery ASE object is coordinates plus bookkeeping. Doing the geometry by hand once proves there is nothing hidden in there.

02
Build copper, then derive its density

Build fcc copper at a = 3.61 Å twice — the primitive cell and the cubic cell. Count the atoms in each, get the volumes, and compute the mass density in g cm−3. Compare with the handbook value of 8.96.

Look up ase.build.bulkcubic=Trueget_volumeget_massesatoms.cell

Checkpoint

Primitive: 1 atom, 11.761 Å³ · Cubic: 4 atoms, 47.05 Å³ · density 8.97 g cm⁻³ either way

If you're stuck

Unit conversion: 1 amu Å−3 = 1.66054 g cm−3.

The density must come out identical for both cells. If it doesn't, you counted atoms in one cell and took the volume of the other.

The teachable momentBeginners assume "the unit cell" is one thing. Two cells, four times the atoms, four times the volume, same density — that lands the concept in thirty seconds on camera.

03
Supercells, slicing, and deleting atoms

Repeat the cubic Cu cell 3×3×3. Count the atoms and check the new cell lengths. Then: delete atom 0; select every atom with z < 4 Å; make a mixed Cu–Au system by changing the symbols of ten atoms; and translate everything by half a lattice vector and wrap it back into the box.

Look up atoms * (3,3,3)del atoms[i]atoms[mask]set_chemical_symbolstranslatewrap

Checkpoint

108 atoms · cell lengths 10.83 Å in all three directions

If you're stuck

atoms.positions[:, 2] < 4 gives you a boolean mask you can index the Atoms object with directly.

After translate, some atoms sit outside the cell. wrap() folds them back. Nothing physical changed — verify by checking the potential energy is unaffected.

The teachable momentThat wrap check — same energy, different coordinates — is the cleanest possible demonstration of what periodicity means, and it takes one line.

04
Write and read four file formats

Take your 32-atom Cu cell and round-trip it through .xyz, .cif, .traj and a VASP POSCAR. After each read-back, verify the atom count and that the cell survived. Then find out what plain .xyz loses that extxyz keeps.

Look up ase.io.writease.io.readformat='extxyz'index=':'

Checkpoint

All four give back 32 atoms with the cell intact — as long as you write extxyz, not bare xyz. Bare .xyz has no place to store a cell, so the round-trip silently returns a non-periodic cluster.

If you're stuck

np.allclose(back.cell, original.cell) is your check. When it fails for plain xyz, that's the answer, not a bug.

read(fn) returns the last frame; read(fn, index=':') returns a list of all of them. That distinction bites everyone exactly once.

The teachable momentSilent data loss on a format nobody warned them about. Demonstrating it once inoculates your viewers against a bug that can survive into a paper.

Tier 2

Calculators, and getting real numbers

4 drills · ~3 hrs

Where ASE stops being a structure editor. By the end of this tier you will have reproduced two measured properties of copper from nothing but a potential — that's the moment the tool becomes credible.

05
Your first energy and force

Attach the EMT calculator to a 2×2×2 supercell of cubic Cu. Get the potential energy, the energy per atom, and the forces. Explain the force result. Then displace one atom by 0.1 Å and look again.

Look up ase.calculators.emt.EMTatoms.calc = …get_potential_energyget_forces

Checkpoint

32 atoms · −0.0057 eV/atom · every force component exactly 0.0 — not small, but bit-for-bit zero. Work out why before you read the hint.

If you're stuck

Every atom in a perfect fcc crystal sits at a centre of inversion symmetry. For every neighbour pulling one way there is an identical neighbour pulling exactly the opposite way, and the contributions cancel in floating point too.

Displace one atom and the symmetry breaks: that atom and its neighbours all pick up non-zero forces, and the total force over the whole cell still sums to zero (Newton's third law).

The teachable momentExactly-zero forces look like a broken calculator to a beginner. Turning it into a symmetry argument, then breaking it deliberately, is a complete five-minute segment.

06
Scan the Lennard-Jones dimer curve

Two argon atoms, separation from 3.0 to 6.0 Å. Compute the energy at each separation with ASE's LennardJones (ε = 0.0104 eV, σ = 3.40 Å) and plot it. Find the minimum numerically and check both its position and its depth against the analytic predictions.

Look up ase.Atomscalculators.lj.LennardJonesepsilon, sigma, rcnp.argmin

Checkpoint

Minimum at 3.82 Å, matching 21/6σ = 3.816 Å · depth −0.0104 eV, exactly −ε

If you're stuck

Set rc well beyond your largest separation (say 15 Å) or the cutoff shift will move your well depth and you'll chase a phantom error.

Build each dimer as Atoms('Ar2', positions=[[0,0,0],[r,0,0]]) with no cell and no pbc.

The teachable momentTwo parameters you typed in, two numbers that come back out with the textbook relationship between them. That is what "the potential is the physics" means, shown rather than said.

07
Cohesive energy of copper

How much energy does it cost to pull one copper atom out of the crystal to infinity? Compute the bulk energy per atom, then the energy of a single isolated Cu atom in a large empty box, and take the difference. Compare with the experimental 3.49 eV/atom.

Look up bulk('Cu')Atoms('Cu', cell=…, pbc=False)get_potential_energy

Checkpoint

3.516 eV/atom against an experimental 3.49 eV/atom — under 1% error from a potential you can read in an afternoon

If you're stuck

The isolated atom needs pbc=False and a cell big enough that it can't see its own images — 12 Å is plenty for EMT.

Sign convention: cohesive energy is defined positive, so it's E(isolated) − E(bulk per atom).

The teachable momentThis is your first genuine prediction — a measured property of a real metal, recovered on a laptop in eight lines. Put the number next to the handbook value on screen.

08
Equation of state: lattice constant and bulk modulus

Scan the lattice constant from 3.4 to 3.8 Å in nine steps, recording volume and energy at each. Fit an equation of state and extract the equilibrium volume, the equilibrium lattice constant, and the bulk modulus in GPa. Plot the fit over your points.

Look up ase.eos.EquationOfStateeos.fit()eos.plot()ase.units.kJ

Checkpoint

V0 = 11.57 Å³ · a0 = 3.590 Å (expt 3.61) · B = 133 GPa (expt ~140)

If you're stuck

For fcc, the primitive volume is a³/4, so a0 = (4 * v0) ** (1/3).

The unit conversion for the modulus is B / kJ * 1e24 with from ase.units import kJ. Getting a number near 10−3 means you skipped it.

The teachable momentTwo independent properties from one nine-point scan — and both land within a few percent. This is the drill that convinces a sceptical viewer the whole enterprise is real.

Tier 3

Structures, defects, and relaxation

4 drills · ~4 hrs

Everything a real simulation needs before the dynamics start. This tier is also where your own research vocabulary — surfaces, adsorption, defects — first appears in the workbook.

09
Build and relax a Cu(111) slab

Make a 3-layer Cu(111) slab, 2×2 in-plane, with 8 Å of vacuum. Freeze everything below the top layer using the layer tags, then relax with BFGS to fmax=0.05. Measure how much the top interlayer spacing changed from the bulk value.

Look up ase.build.fcc111vacuum=atom.tagconstraints.FixAtomsoptimize.BFGS

Checkpoint

12 atoms for size (2,2,3) · the top layer relaxes inward, contracting the first interlayer spacing by roughly a percent

If you're stuck

fcc111 tags layers 1, 2, 3 from the top. So FixAtoms(mask=[a.tag > 1 for a in slab]) frees only the surface layer.

Pass logfile=None to BFGS to silence it, or leave it on — watching fmax fall is worth showing on camera.

The teachable momentSurface relaxation is a real, measurable phenomenon that beginners have never heard of, and it falls out of a five-line script. Excellent episode material.

10
Binding energy of an adatom

Put a single Cu adatom on the fcc hollow site of your relaxed slab, 2 Å above the surface. Relax again. Compute the binding energy as E(slab+adatom) − E(slab) − E(isolated atom). Then repeat for the hcp, bridge and ontop sites and rank them.

Look up ase.build.add_adsorbateposition='fcc'height=

Checkpoint

fcc hollow: −2.79 eV. Negative means bound. The hollow sites should beat bridge, which should beat ontop — more neighbours, stronger binding.

If you're stuck

Re-apply the FixAtoms constraint after add_adsorbate — copying the slab does not always carry it, and an unconstrained slab will drift and ruin the energy difference.

All three energies must use the same calculator settings, or the difference is meaningless.

The teachable momentAn energy difference between two calculations is the workhorse of computational materials science. Site ranking makes the abstraction concrete and gives viewers something to predict before you run it.

11
Vacancy formation energy

Take a 3×3×3 supercell of cubic Cu (108 atoms), record its energy, then delete one atom and relax. The formation energy is E(defective) − E(perfect) × (N−1)/N. Compare with the experimental ~1.28 eV. Then check how much the answer moves if you use a 2×2×2 cell instead.

Look up del atoms[0]BFGS(fmax=0.02)len(atoms)

Checkpoint

108 atoms → 1.244 eV against experiment's ~1.28 eV. The smaller cell gives a visibly different answer.

If you're stuck

That (N−1)/N scaling is the whole trick — you are comparing systems with different atom counts, so you must reference against the same number of atoms' worth of perfect crystal.

The small-cell discrepancy is the vacancy interacting with its own periodic images. Finite-size effects, demonstrated rather than asserted.

The teachable momentConvergence with system size is the single most under-taught idea in beginner MD. Two runs, two different answers, one lesson: always check that your box is big enough.

12
Nanoparticles and neighbour lists

Build a 3-shell Cu icosahedron and a truncated octahedron. Then use ASE's neighbour list on bulk Cu to compute the average coordination number and the nearest-neighbour distance. Finally, compute the coordination of every atom in the nanoparticle and colour-code surface versus interior.

Look up ase.cluster.Icosahedronase.cluster.Octahedronneighborlist.neighbor_listnp.bincount

Checkpoint

Icosahedron(3 shells) = 55 atoms · Octahedron(length 5, cutoff 1) = 79 atoms · bulk coordination 12 · nn distance 2.5527 Å = a/√2

If you're stuck

i, j, d = neighbor_list('ijd', atoms, cutoff=3.0) returns flat arrays of pairs and distances. np.bincount(i) counts each atom's neighbours.

Pick your cutoff between the first and second neighbour shells — plot the RDF first if you're unsure where that is.

The teachable moment55 is a magic number, and the nearest-neighbour distance comes out as a/√2 to four decimal places. Both are checkable by hand, which is exactly what makes them convincing.

Tier 4

Limits, and the bridge to dynamics

2 drills · ~2 hrs

The last two drills are the ones that make you a trustworthy teacher rather than a confident one.

13
Break your calculator on purpose

EMT was fitted for close-packed metals. Point it at an N2 molecule anyway: relax the bond, then compute the vibrational frequency with ASE's Vibrations. Compare both against experiment (1.098 Å, 2359 cm−1). Then work out how you would have known this was a bad idea before running it.

Look up ase.vibrations.Vibrationsvib.run()vib.summary()get_energies

Checkpoint

Bond 0.998 Å versus 1.098 · stretch 1231 cm⁻¹ versus 2359. Off by nearly a factor of two, with no error, no warning, and a perfectly clean-looking output.

If you're stuck

Vibrations writes cache files to a directory. Delete it between runs (shutil.rmtree('vib')) or you'll silently reuse stale results — a good bug to demonstrate in its own right.

Convert energies to wavenumbers with the factor 8065.54 cm−1 per eV.

How you'd know in advance: read what the potential was fitted to. EMT's own documentation lists its supported elements and its intended regime, and diatomic nitrogen is nowhere in it.

Why this is the most important drillA confidently wrong number with no warning attached is the defining failure mode of computational science, and almost no free tutorial shows one. Making this an episode is the single strongest signal you can send that your channel is worth trusting.

14
Set something in motion

The bridge into the series proper. Take your Cu supercell, give the atoms velocities from a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution at 300 K, and integrate with velocity Verlet for 200 steps at 1 fs, printing energy and temperature every 10 steps. Then answer one question: why does the temperature immediately fall to roughly half of what you asked for?

Look up md.velocitydistribution.thermalize_momentamd.verlet.VelocityVerletase.units.fsdyn.attachget_temperature

Checkpoint

Total energy should stay flat to about 0.006 meV/atom over the run. Temperature starts near 300 K and settles near 150 K.

If you're stuck

Note the modern spelling: thermalize_momenta replaced MaxwellBoltzmannDistribution in ASE 3.29, and ase.md.npt.NPT moved to ase.md.melchionna.MelchionnaNPT. Older tutorials will show you the old names.

timestep=1.0 * units.fs — ASE's internal time unit is not the femtosecond, and forgetting the conversion is the classic first MD bug.

The temperature halves because you put all the energy in as kinetic. Equipartition then splits it evenly between kinetic and potential, so the kinetic half — which is what temperature measures — drops to 50%. Start at 600 K if you want to end near 300 K.

Where this leadsThis is episode 10 of the series, and every drill above it is now a prerequisite you've personally done. You are ready to record.

When all fourteen are ticked, you'll have reproduced four measured properties of copper, broken a calculator on purpose, and run your first trajectory — which is more hands-on ASE than most people have when they start a PhD project with it. At that point record episodes 1 through 12 back to back, because you'll have lived every error your viewers are about to hit.
